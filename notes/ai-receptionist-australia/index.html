AI receptionist for Australian SMBs: what it is, what it costs

An AI receptionist is a voice agent that answers the phone, captures the job, and books it — at 9pm, in the middle of a job, and on the fourth simultaneous call.

Johan Iskandar · 3 August 2026

An AI receptionist is a voice agent that answers your business phone: it picks up on the first ring, holds a natural conversation, captures the caller and job details, books the appointment in your calendar, logs the lead in your CRM, and escalates anything outside its rules to a person. It costs a fixed build plus roughly A$50 to A$300 a month to run as an owned system (market figures), against a receptionist salary or a per-minute answering service.

It is not a phone menu, and it is not voicemail with better manners. The test is whether the call ends with a booking in your calendar and a record in your CRM without anyone touching it.

What it actually does on a call

StepWhat happens
AnswersPicks up every call, including the fourth simultaneous one and the 9pm one
QualifiesAsks your questions in your order — job type, location, urgency, budget signal
BooksChecks live calendar availability, offers real slots, writes the appointment
LogsCreates the lead in your CRM with the transcript and captured fields attached
EscalatesTransfers, or flags for callback, anything outside its rules
Follows upSends the confirmation and the reminder without anyone remembering to

The escalation row is the one to interrogate when you are buying. An agent that answers everything confidently is a liability; an agent that knows the edge of its rules and hands over cleanly is the product.

What it costs, honestly

There are two buying structures, and they behave very differently over three years. Subscription voice tools charge per minute or per seat on a vendor plan — fast to start, and you are renting the system for as long as you use it. An owned build is a fixed price quoted in writing after scoping, then the running cost of the models and telephony underneath it, spec'd up front. The full structure sits on the pricing page, and the ownership trade-off — renting a vendor's system versus owning yours — is the one to settle before you sign anything.

Either way, the number that decides it is not the monthly fee. It is what the calls you currently miss are worth — the arithmetic in what a missed call costs an Australian service business. If two recovered jobs a month cover the running cost, the decision makes itself.

When a human receptionist still wins

Complex, sensitive, or high-variance calls. A distressed client, a negotiation, an account problem with history behind it — those need a person, and pretending otherwise costs more than the automation saves. Low call volume also kills the case: if you miss two calls a month, this is not your bottleneck.

The common outcome is a split. The agent takes after-hours, overflow, and repeat first-touch calls; the person takes the calls that need judgment and finally gets time for the work a phone-tethered receptionist never had room for. First-touch intake with strict escalation rules is exactly how legal intake automation is scoped, for the same reason: the capture is mechanical, the advice is not.

How to buy one without regretting it

Ask for a recording of the agent handling a call it cannot answer. Ask where the data lives and whose accounts it runs in. Ask what happens on day one if it books a job wrong — the answer should be that it runs supervised until it has earned autonomy, which is the process in how an agent earns autonomy. Then ask for the running cost in writing before the build, not after.

Disclose the AI to callers, keep the escalation number live, and review transcripts weekly for the first month. Do that and the phone stops being the thing that leaks work while you are busy doing it.
